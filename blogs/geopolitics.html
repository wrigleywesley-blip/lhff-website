LHFF × MOZ' BRI: Mozambique, the Belt and Road, and what young Africans stand to inherit.

A new port, a new road, a new rail line. The infrastructure you build in your twenties is the country you live in for the rest of your life. The Foundation works with young Mozambicans who will inherit decisions being made now, with very little of their input.

The Belt and Road Initiative reaches Mozambique through ports, railways, and energy projects. The headlines are mostly about debt and influence. The headlines are mostly written outside the country. The young people who will live in the Mozambique that emerges from these projects are rarely in the room when the projects are designed.

This is not a piece for or against Chinese investment. It is a piece about who gets to decide. The Foundation's interest in this work is narrow and specific. We are not a think tank. We are not a development agency. We work with young people in Maputo and Cape Town who have to carry the inheritance. Our research exists to make that inheritance legible to them.

What is actually being built

The Mozambican BRI footprint includes port upgrades, road corridors connecting Mozambique to inland neighbours, and energy infrastructure. Some of the work is good. The N1 has been improved in stretches that were genuinely impassable. Port capacity has expanded. The grid has held in places where it would otherwise have failed.

Some of the work is more complicated. The terms of the financing are often opaque. The labour mix on the largest projects skews toward imported workers rather than local hire. Environmental assessments are uneven. And the question of what happens to assets if Mozambique cannot service the debt is, in places, a question the public answer to has not been fully published.

None of this is hidden. It is just diffuse. Pieces of it sit in different ministries, different reports, different languages. The single biggest gap is not information. The gap is synthesis.

What this means for the young people we work with

The young Mozambicans in our retreats and curriculum are between fifteen and twenty-eight. By the time they are forty, the country they live in will be shaped by infrastructure decisions that are being signed off this decade. Our argument is that they should know what those decisions are, in plain language, while there is still time to influence them.

That sounds obvious. It is not how most education systems work. Schools teach history, sometimes civics, occasionally economics. Very few teach the specific shape of the country that is being built around the students in real time. The Foundation's role is partly to fill that gap, in the small communities we touch.

What we publish, and why

We publish a short briefing, in English and Portuguese, twice a year. It summarises the publicly available information on each major BRI project in Mozambique. It does not editorialise. It does not pretend to be neutral, either. The framing is openly that of a foundation that works with young Africans and wants them informed.

The briefing is used in the 7 Pillars curriculum, in the corporate retreats, and by partner schools in Maputo. We share it on request with anyone working in education or civil society in southern Africa. It is not a campaign document. It is a study aid.

The next twelve months

We are expanding the briefing to cover three additional countries in the SADC region where comparable infrastructure programmes are running. We are also building a small public-facing version, in plain language, for use outside formal education. The aim is the same as the original work. Make the inheritance legible. Let the people who will live in it see what is being built.

If you work in this space and want to compare notes, write to us. We are particularly interested in collaborators in Maputo, Beira, and Tete who can ground-truth what we publish.
