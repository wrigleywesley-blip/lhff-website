Organic Seeds: food, sovereignty, and the school garden as civic infrastructure.

A school garden is not a decoration. It is a quiet political act. The seeds you plant, the hands that plant them, and the children who carry the harvest home make a community that knows where its food comes from. The Foundation works in that field, literally and figuratively.

The organic seeds programme started small. A teacher in a Maputo primary school asked us a question that did not feel small. Where do these children think food comes from? Most of them, she said, would tell you it comes from a packet. From a shop. From the city. None of them would tell you it comes from soil they could touch.

That gap, between a child and their food, is one of the quieter inheritances of the last fifty years. Industrial agriculture solved supply. It also broke the relationship. In southern Africa the break is sharper because the landscape is still close, the seasons still felt, the markets still local. Reconnecting one to the other is not a nostalgia project. It is a sovereignty project.

What seed sovereignty actually means

Seeds sit at the start of every food system. Whoever owns the seed, owns the meal. For most of human history, seeds were saved, traded, and replanted by the people who grew the food. Over the last three decades that has shifted. A handful of corporations now hold patents on the genetics of the world's most-eaten crops. Farmers re-buy what their grandparents grew freely.

The Foundation's organic seeds initiative is built around the opposite proposition. Open-pollinated, locally adapted seeds, saved at the school site, traded between schools, and replanted year after year. It is slow, it is unglamorous, and it produces a different relationship to the land. The children who grow up planting and saving seed do not need to be taught what sovereignty means. They have already practised it.

Why the school garden is the right unit

We chose schools, rather than farms, because the school is the natural civic space in most of the communities we work in. A school garden does five things at once. It teaches biology, agronomy, and nutrition. It feeds the school. It pulls parents into the school grounds. It produces a small surplus that can go to the wider community. And it gives the school a reason to exist beyond the classroom.

The framework we use is deliberately simple. Each partner school receives a seed library, a trained teacher, three years of curriculum support, and a connection to a wider network of school gardens across the region. The aim is not to produce expert farmers. It is to produce children who understand where they live.

What we have learned

Three things, in three years.

First, school gardens fail when they are projects. They succeed when they become rhythms. The schools that have made the practice stick are the ones where the garden has become part of the timetable, not a special extra-curricular event. The shift is small but everything turns on it.

Second, the harvest is the easy part. The hard part is the wait. Children who have grown up on instant feedback need a different relationship with time when they plant a seed. That patience, that we-will-see-in-three-months patience, is its own kind of education.

Third, the parents matter more than we expected. A garden that the parents come to weed on a Saturday becomes a different kind of garden. The relationship between school and household, often stretched in our partner communities, finds a new place to anchor.

Where it goes

The next phase opens the seed library across all four current partner schools, formalises the teacher-to-teacher network, and adds a fifth and sixth school in 2026. We are also writing a longer field manual that other schools, outside our partnership, can adopt independently. Sovereignty does not scale by holding the work close. It scales by making it easy to copy.

If you are working in education, food systems, or rural development in southern Africa and want to compare notes, we would be glad to talk. The work is open. It exists to be passed on.
