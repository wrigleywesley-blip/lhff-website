The Model Protection Framework: towards legal dignity for young women in fashion.

A young woman walking into a casting room in Cape Town, Lagos, or Maputo is protected by almost nothing. The Foundation has spent two years writing a working framework for what protection should look like. This is the short version.

Fashion is one of the largest creative industries in the world. It is also one of the most lightly regulated, especially at the start of a model's career. The first jobs, the first castings, the first shoots, the first contracts. The early years where most of the harm happens.

The Foundation's interest is narrow. We do not regulate the industry. We are not a union. We do, however, work closely with young women who model. We see what happens to them. We have taken the time to write down what we believe basic protection should look like. The framework is offered openly to agencies, photographers, brands, and lawmakers who want to use it.

What is missing

Five things, repeatedly.

First, there is rarely a written contract for early work. A girl shows up to a shoot with a verbal agreement, a memory of what was promised, and no document she can hold afterwards.

Second, age is often unverified or, worse, deliberately misrepresented. The industry has been on notice about this for decades. The notice has not produced enough change.

Third, image rights and usage are routinely ambiguous. A photograph taken for a portfolio appears in a campaign. The model is not paid. There is no contract that defines the boundary, so there is no breach.

Fourth, working conditions on set, especially around chaperoning, breaks, food, and travel, are not standard. They depend on the specific people in the room.

Fifth, there is no independent reporting channel. A young woman who has had a bad experience has nowhere obvious to take the complaint that does not put her future work at risk.

What the framework proposes

The framework is not a law. It is a checklist that any agency, photographer, or brand can adopt voluntarily, signed at the start of any working relationship with a model under the age of twenty-five.

It covers six areas. Written contracts before the first paid job. Verified age and a clear minimum age for specific kinds of work. Defined image rights with explicit usage scope and renewal. On-set standards for chaperoning, breaks, food, and travel. A named, independent contact for concerns who is outside the agency. And an annual review with the model, in writing, that captures how the year has actually gone.

None of this is radical. Most of it would be standard in any other industry where young people work. Fashion has been an exception.

Who is using it

The framework is currently in voluntary use by a small group of agencies and photographers in Cape Town and Maputo. The Foundation does not certify or audit. We publish, advocate, and support. The adoption is the agency's responsibility.

We have also begun conversations with industry bodies in two African markets about a regional version of the framework. Those conversations are early and we will publish more when there is something concrete to share.

What this is, and what it is not

This is not an attempt to police the industry. It is a written record of what the Foundation believes basic dignity in fashion looks like, offered openly, used voluntarily, refined over time. We do not own it. Anyone who works with young models can adopt it tomorrow. We will help if asked.

If you are a model, agency, brand, photographer, or lawmaker and want to discuss the framework, we would be glad to talk. The full document is available on request. The work is meant to be passed around.
